# Editorial visual system

Read this for video/live/HTML decks, data-led PPTs, or whenever the brief asks to avoid generic AI-slide styling.

## Start with a reading contract

Choose one primary reading speed before picking a layout:

| Mode | Viewer behavior | Visual grammar | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glance | Understand the point in 3–10 seconds | Few large forms, explicit ranking/comparison, direct labels | Pitch, weekly review, a spoken presentation beat |
| Editorial | Spend 30+ seconds examining evidence | Real units, fine rules, annotations, measured whitespace | Research, a proof slide, portfolio, a close-read moment |

Use Glance for a fast decision, not as a default. Use Editorial only when the audience has the time and the material merits inspection. A deck can switch modes at a deliberate section boundary, but a single page has one mode.

## Make visual form earn its place

1. State the conclusion in the headline. Name the implication, not the chart type.
2. Select a chart or diagram from the data shape: comparison, composition, time, distribution, relationship, flow, or hierarchy. Never choose by decoration.
3. Preserve the data contract: position and length correspond to values; area is proportional to value; labels fit or move to a callout; axes are not misleadingly truncated.
4. Treat the headline, unit, annotation, source, and whitespace as part of the information design. They make a chart legible; they are not afterthoughts.
5. One visual earns one claim. Split competing claims instead of putting six unrelated widgets on a slide.

For a sparse but real dataset, add context—guide lines, units, source, comparison baseline, or a relevant annotation—not invented data points.

## Build a deck-specific visual vocabulary

Before scaling beyond three prototype slides, document:

- **Material cue:** one visual source from the subject (e.g. a course timetable, printed invoice, laboratory notebook, theatre program, campus noticeboard). It should affect composition or texture, not become pasted-on decoration.
- **Type behavior:** one display/body pairing and a rule for Chinese/English hierarchy.
- **Color behavior:** a restrained base plus one semantic accent. Use color to distinguish meaning, not merely to make each page feel new.
- **Structural motifs:** 2–3 repeatable layouts or mark types. Repetition is a system; every slide being different is not originality.
- **Hero exception:** reserve one visually exceptional moment for the core proof, payoff, or pivot.

Avoid unmotivated gradients, glow, glass cards, uniform rounded rectangles, random blobs, stock-device mockups, and ornamental motion. They are permissible only when the brand, source, or subject makes them meaningful.

## Motion

Use motion to reveal order, comparison, or cause. It must not require a new decorative layer, and the static frame must still communicate the point. Provide a reduced-motion or export-safe fallback whenever the format supports it.

## Anti-homogenization check

Before delivery, inspect the exported deck and answer:

1. Could this deck be relabeled from its actual subject to "AI productivity" without altering the design? If yes, replace generic motifs with a subject-derived material cue.
2. Is the main visual claim supported by real content, or could it be deleted without changing the message? Delete or rebuild decorative visuals.
3. Do colors encode a stable role across the deck? If not, lock the palette.
4. Are all pages constructed from the same card-grid template? Retain intentional repetition but introduce hierarchy through composition, scale, crop, or evidence—not random styling.
5. Is the result clearer at actual presentation size? If not, reduce content or change the encoding; never shrink type to rescue it.

The target is not novelty for its own sake. The target is a coherent visual language that belongs to this content and makes the audience understand it faster or more deeply.
